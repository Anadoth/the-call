# The Call

Two people pursuing God together — knowing Him more deeply and, more deeply still, being known by Him — through real friction with each other, "as iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). Knowing yourself and your partner is part means, part byproduct; the goal is God.

**In brief**

If you're worn out drinking from things that never satisfy — the feed, the metric, the next thing — you are called by God, to God.

Two people meet on a standing cadence — once a month is a good default, kept on the calendar — and pursue God out loud together: talking about their real wrestling with the world, with their own lives, with God, and letting that pursuit bear on the actual events of their days. Each holds themselves to their own values and invites the other to do the same.

The unit is a **dyad**: two people, and only two. A dyad cannot hide — no third party to perform for, no crowd to soften a hard truth into consensus — and it is the only unit with the bandwidth for real depth. When you want more, you add dyads, not people to the room.

The posture is witness, not verdict. You hold yourself to your own values first and come as a fellow traveler, not an inspector. You testify to what you see — "here is what I noticed, here is how it landed on me" — and never pronounce on the other's motives or standing before God. And you do not force: you offer what you see and leave the response to the other and to God, as the father in the parable of the prodigal son watched the road and ran to his son only when the son turned home.

This is a shared search, not a contest to convince. You may hold very different beliefs and keep holding them; the aim is not to win the other over but to help each other see further and live more honestly than either could alone.

That openness extends to atheists and agnostics who are earnestly seeking truth and find the spirit of The Call appealing even if they disagree with the explicitly Christian framing of this practice. The Call was inspired by a friendship between a Christian and an atheist. Neither needs the other to convert. Both value the shared pursuit of truth.

This is the core of a living practice. Some extensions are described in the rest of this document, and more will be discovered.

## The core

Life often feels unsatisfying. Jesus offered living water, inexhaustible satisfaction. Life often feels lonely. Paul offered a relationship with a God who knows you deeper than you do. Life often feels like it's missing something — like it's built on the wrong things. God offers the one aim worth pursuing, and even boasting about: knowing Him (Jeremiah 9:23–24).

But how do you get there?

The Call is an ancient phrase for that longing to get there — God calling us to Himself.

The Call is also a practice for answering this longing, typically through a phone call with a fellow traveler. Together, the two of you are a dyad.

**The calls are regular, and on the calendar.** This is a standing commitment, not an occasional catch-up when you both happen to have time — the regularity is part of what makes it formation rather than friendship-in-general. The interval is flexible and chosen to suit the dyad; **once a month is a good default.** Put it on the calendar and keep it there. The rhythm is what lets the pursuit accumulate — each call picking up what the last one surfaced, the accountability having somewhere to land on a known cadence.

**What the two of you do:** talk about your real wrestling — with the world, with your own life, with God — in order to share the journey and help each other make progress. You pursue God together, out loud, and you let that pursuit bear on the actual events of your days. God is the center because God is the one goal that outlives every circumstance — unreachable, so the pursuit never ends and never expires. There is an answer; we cannot know it completely; we can always do better.

**And you hold each other accountable to your own stated values** — each person to *their own*, not to a shared standard imposed from outside. At the start this can be entirely verbal: you say what you are trying to be and how you are trying to live, in whatever rough and evolving words you have, and you invite the other to hold you to it. Writing it down is an option for later (see below); the accountability is the core, and it works from day one in spoken form. You are unvarnished with each other, and you receive the other's honesty as a gift rather than a threat. Full resistance, permanent membership: you push on each other freely, and none of it touches whether you stay.

**This is a shared pursuit of truth, not a debate.** The aim is not to win the other over to your account of things — it is for two people to seek what is true and good together, each helping the other see further and live more honestly than they could alone. You may hold very different beliefs and keep holding them; agreement was never the point. The first dyad this practice grew from is between a Christian and an atheist. After twenty years of exchange, both still hold their positions — and both regard the shared pursuit as one of the most valuable things in their lives, precisely because of how the other has refined them. Neither converted the other; both were sharpened by the other. That is the mode: companions in a search, not opponents in an argument. The moment it becomes a contest to convince, it has stopped being The Call.

**How you hold each other — the part that keeps this from going wrong.** Accountability between two people is dangerous equipment; used badly it becomes mutual judgment. So the practice has a spine:

- **Hold yourself to your own values first.** The posture is not "I will keep you honest" but "I am trying to live up to what I've said, and I want your help — and I offer you the same." You come as a fellow traveler, not an inspector.
- **Witness, not verdict.** You testify to what *you* see and experience — "here is what I noticed, here is how that landed on me" — never pronouncing on the other's motives, heart, or standing before God. You cannot see another person's interior; you can only report your own and describe what is on the surface between you. Judging a pattern of conduct against a standard the other person has *themselves* stated is fair and is the whole point; judging *who they are inside* is the drift to refuse.
- **No forcing.** You do not corner the other into a conclusion or a confession. You offer what you see and leave the response to them and to God — the way the father in the parable of the prodigal son watched the road and ran only when the son turned, rather than chasing him into the far country.

That is the entire core: two people, meeting on a regular cadence, pursuing God out loud, holding themselves and each other to their own values, as witnesses rather than judges. Everything that follows is a way of going deeper — never a requirement for having begun.

## Why the dyad

A core premise of The Call is that a dyad is optimal for deep spiritual formation. God is present in a different way than in individual prayer (Matthew 18:20). And two people engaged in conversation are maximally present to each other. They can have high-bandwidth, focused engagement that is optimized to the dyad, not the lowest common denominator in a larger group. There is room for honesty and accountability that is difficult to match in a group setting.

Larger group meetings have value of their own and can complement participation in one or more dyads, but they can't replace the power of the dyad. One or more parties can check out or be excluded by the loudest voices without anyone else noticing. The allure of conforming to the group can stifle discussion. You cannot simultaneously hold five people in one room to their deepest commitments with the attention you can give to one. A group of five does not give you a fifth of the depth; it gives you a different thing altogether, where the hard truths get diluted into consensus and no one is truly held.

The greatest depth lives in the dyad. When you want more, you add more dyads — see below — not more people to the room.

## The extensions (optional, and roughly in order)

These build on the core. The first stands alone; the middle two form a sequence, each depending on the one before; the last depends on having written a creed. Take them as far as fits — there is no level to reach and no one keeping count.

### 1. Add more dyads (independent of everything below)

One dyad is the practice. Having **more than one** is better — not because it scales you (it doesn't; each dyad stays singular and deep, and depth is exactly the part that must not scale), but for a specific and humbling reason: **it keeps you from over-relying on any one flawed person.** Every partner, including you, brings blind spots, bad days, and their own distortions. A single dyad can quietly become an echo chamber, or a comfort that stops challenging, or the one relationship you route everything through until its particular flaws become your own. Several relationships guard against that — and they give you somewhere to go for help if problems arise in one dyad.

**Recommendation: keep your dyads non-overlapping.** Two partners who do not know each other is better than a triangle where all three do. A triangle collapses back toward a group — there is a third party in the room by implication, hard things can be triangulated, and the dyad's freedom from an audience erodes. Separate, non-overlapping dyads preserve what makes the dyad work: each stays a closed, private, unhidable space of two.

### 2. Write your own creed (depends on: the core)

Take the values you've been holding yourself to verbally and **write them down** — a plain statement of what you believe, what you are for, and what you are trying to become. This is not adopting someone else's creed; it is formalizing *your own*, making durable and precise the thing the core already contained in rough spoken form. It stays a living document; you revise it as you grow. A written creed gives the accountability a firmer surface: something specific you can be held to, and something you can watch change over the years.

### 3. Share your creed (depends on: writing it)

Once written, **share your creed** with a dyad partner — so they can hold you to *your own stated* commitments with precision, and so their honest reading can help you refine it. Sharing sharpens both the accountability and the creed itself. You can share with more than one partner; each will see it differently, and each reading is a gift.

Ideally, sharing becomes a **mutual swap** — each of you holding the other to the other's own deepest commitments, the most intimate form of the exchange. That swap comes late if it comes, as the fruit of earned trust rather than the mechanism for manufacturing it. But it is *not* required: a dyad can run well on asymmetry, one creed shared and the other not, as long as both are at peace with that. Depth is the precondition for a swap, never the goal, and plenty of good dyads never reach or need it.

### 4. Add an AI companion — strictly subordinate (depends on: writing a creed)

Once you have a written creed, you *may* enlist an AI assistant as a kind of practice partner: give it your creed and let it help with your personal development between your human calls — noticing patterns in what you write, surfacing tensions, pointing you toward your sacred texts and toward God, helping you prepare what you want to bring to a partner. Used well, it can tend your interior between conversations and help you arrive at your human calls more ready.

**But it is subordinate, always, and the subordination is not a footnote — it is the condition of using it at all:**

- **The human relationships take precedence, without exception.** The AI is never a dyad partner; it is a tool that assists *you* between dyads. When anything it offers competes with a human call — for your time, your trust, or your vulnerability — the human call wins.
- **It points you back to people, not into itself.** Its job is to return you to the living encounter — with God, and with the person across from you in your dyad — never to become the place your formation lands. If you find yourself bringing to the AI what belongs in a dyad, that is the signal to close it and call your partner.
- **Know its dangers.** An AI responds instantly, tirelessly, and agreeably — which makes it very good at producing the *feeling* of being understood while the real, slower, costlier work of being known by a person quietly atrophies. It can train you to treat your own soul as a map to be managed rather than a life to be lived. It has no self to push back from and no stake in the truth. These are not reasons never to use it; they are reasons to use it *as a subordinate tool inside a human practice*, and to be honest with yourself the moment it starts becoming the center. The practice is complete without it. It is an enrichment, not a pillar.

## How it spreads

By contagion, never by quota. No one is required to recruit; that is the machinery of a pyramid, and people can smell it. Instead, the practice is built so that being in a good Call relationship naturally makes you want to offer it to someone. If a few people each tend a few dyads, and those dyads quietly seed their own, there is more than enough room for it to spread — without a center anyone could capture, own, or corrupt.

The dyads typically form among people already in your life with some shared seriousness — a reconnected old friend, a collaborator, someone who mentioned the same longing you feel. Finding them is slow and takes real searching; that difficulty is a feature, not a flaw. It means you arrive ready for depth rather than shopping for it.

## The Call and the Church

A word on how The Call relates to the Church, because the practice raises the question immediately.

The distinction that matters is between **the body of Christ — the real Church** — and the **institutions called "church,"** which are *one way* of being that body, not the only way and not one guaranteed to last for the rest of human history. The thing to maintain is the body of Christ. The institutional forms are vehicles for it; they have served that end and can serve it well, but they are means, not the thing itself, and no particular institution is owed permanence.

Held that way, The Call is simply **another way of being the body of Christ** — small, personal, accountable. In an ideal world it would sit *alongside* a healthy congregation, each strengthening the other. But much of what motivated the launch of The Call — first informally, then as something more formalized — was the brokenness of many institutional churches: many have traded formation for information and belonging for performance. So The Call can also stand fully on its own as a way of keeping faith alive and formation real when the institution has abandoned that work. It is not a lesser substitute for the institution; it is a different vehicle for the same body — one that can complement a congregation, or replace a broken one, without apology in either case.

## What it is not

It is **not a brand**, and its plainness is deliberate — a thing people *do*, not a thing people *join*.

It is **not a single official creed** enforced on everyone. The content stays radically plural, and what is shared across the network is not *what* people believe but *that* they have articulated it and submitted it to at least one other person's loving scrutiny.

It is **not a program with a finish line.** No one graduates from The Call; there is no curriculum to complete, no level to reach. It is an ongoing relationship in an unending pursuit — the way friendship is, the way prayer is.

It is **not a place to judge each other.** The accountability at its core is mutual *witness*, not mutual verdict — each person holding themselves to their own stated values and offering the other honest testimony, never pronouncing on the other's heart or standing before God. A Call relationship that curdles into two people grading each other has become the opposite of the thing. You come as a fellow traveler who is also trying and also failing, not as an inspector.

It is **not a replacement for the body of Christ** — it is one way of *being* that body, alongside others. It can, however, replace a broken *institution* for someone the institution has failed; the real Church is the body, not any particular vehicle for it (see above).

## Why it lasts

A practice built around a reachable goal expires the moment the goal is met or missed.

The Call is built around the one thing that is never finished: knowing and being known by God, approached and never arrived at. Whatever good work the moment asks of us becomes the meaningful expression of that pursuit, not its purpose. The work fits the times. The pursuit outlives them.

The Call represents the oldest equipment a culture forgets it needs until it is gone: a small number of people who know your name, to whom you have made yourself accountable, in pursuit of something worth the trouble.
